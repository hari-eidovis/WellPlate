Executive Summary​
​

The project titled “SpecBridge” is an iOS application that addresses the communication gap
between neurotypical and neurodivergent individuals through experiential learning grounded in
the Double Empathy Problem. System analysis identified that conventional articles, videos, and
questionnaires fail to produce behaviour-changing empathy because they describe rather than
reproduce the experience. The proposed system design follows an MVVM architecture with
three modules: a Sensory Simulator using SwiftUI ViewModifier-based visual effects,
CoreHaptics, and an AVAudioEngine speech-reverb-distortion chain; a Perspective Bridge that
contrasts NT and ND interpretations of identical dialogue; and a Tone Decoder powered by
Apple Foundation Models that generates dual-perspective analysis and three rewrite alternatives.
Implementation was completed in Swift 6 as a Swift Playground App for iOS 18+, with
structured @Generable schemas for AI output and pre-authored fallbacks for unsupported
devices. Testing covered unit, integration, performance, and accessibility dimensions, with the
application sustaining 60 frames per second under all simultaneous filter combinations and
sub-5-millisecond haptic synchronisation. Future scope includes additional sensory profiles,
multilingual support, HealthKit-personalised calibration, and a companion educator dashboard
for classroom deployment.

Abstract

SpecBridge is an iOS application that operationalises the Double Empathy Problem, the
finding that communication breakdowns between neurotypical and neurodivergent
individuals are mutual rather than the result of unilateral deficits, into an interactive,
on-device educational tool. The system combines real-time visual distortion built on the
SwiftUI ViewModifier stack, CoreHaptics patterns, and an algorithmic AVAudioEngine
pipeline (AVAudioSourceNode → AVAudioUnitReverb → AVAudioUnitDistortion) to
deliver sensory simulation across three environment scenes: classroom, grocery store, and
café. A Perspective Bridge module presents dual-perspective dialogue exchanges revealing
how NT and ND readers interpret identical messages differently, and a Tone Decoder
module uses Apple Foundation Models via the @Generable structured-output schema to
analyse user-supplied text and produce three bridge-language rewrites in Direct, Warm, and
Detailed styles. All AI inference runs on-device with graceful degradation to pre-authored
fallback content, ensuring privacy preservation and offline functionality. The application
targets iOS 18+ devices, follows the MVVM architecture, supports VoiceOver, Dynamic
Type, and Reduce Motion, and synthesises every sound at runtime without external audio
assets. Performance testing confirms sustained 60 frames per second under maximum filter
load and sub-second Foundation Models latency on Apple Silicon.

Keywords: Neurodivergent empathy, Double Empathy Problem, iOS, Swift, SwiftUI, Metal,
CoreHaptics, AVAudioEngine, Foundation Models, On-device AI, Accessibility, Sensory
simulation, MVVM, Human–Computer Interaction, Generative AI, Structured output.

Chapter 1: Introduction
1.1 Introduction to the Study
Communication is the substrate on which every workplace, classroom, family, and social
institution is built. When communication succeeds, collaboration flourishes; when it fails,
relationships erode, productivity decays, and individuals experience profound distress. For the
estimated 15–20 percent of the global population that is neurodivergent — a category that
includes individuals on the autism spectrum, those with attention-deficit profiles, dyslexia,
dyspraxia, and a range of related cognitive variations — everyday communication carries an
additional, invisible cost. Subtle social cues, idiomatic language, indirect requests, and unspoken
expectations can become sources of confusion, while the sensory environments in which these
exchanges take place — humming fluorescent lights, overlapping voices, patterned visual stimuli
— may themselves be physically aversive.

Conventional approaches to building empathy between neurotypical and neurodivergent
populations have been overwhelmingly text-based or video-based. Awareness articles describe
the experience of sensory overload in clinical language; documentary videos present interviews
with neurodivergent individuals describing their inner experiences. While these media play a
vital role in shaping cultural awareness, they share a fundamental pedagogical limitation: they
describe rather than reproduce. A neurotypical reader can intellectually acknowledge that a
grocery store may be exhausting for an autistic adult, while still failing to internalise what that
exhaustion actually feels like. The gap between cognitive understanding and embodied empathy
is precisely the gap that perpetuates miscommunication, frustration, and unintended cruelty.

SpecBridge is an attempt to close that gap by means of interactive, multi-sensory simulation.
Rather than telling the user how a neurodivergent person might experience the world, the
application invites the user into a simulated version of that experience: visual fields shimmer and
pulse, ambient noise multiplies and overlays, haptic feedback delivers irregular irritants, and
ordinary tasks — raising one’s hand in class, reading a menu, paying for groceries — become
noticeably harder to complete. The user emerges from the simulation with a felt understanding
that no description alone could provide.

1.2 Background of the Study
The intellectual foundation of this project is the Double Empathy Problem, a framework
proposed by Dr. Damian Milton in 2012 and subsequently refined through more than a decade of
empirical research. The framework argues that miscommunication between neurotypical and
neurodivergent individuals is not the consequence of a one-sided deficit in the neurodivergent
party, as classical theory-of-mind models had assumed, but is instead a mutual breakdown rooted
in genuinely different communicative styles and sensory baselines. Just as neurodivergent people
may struggle to read neurotypical social subtext, neurotypical people struggle equally to read




                                              Page 11
neurodivergent literal speech and may misinterpret directness as rudeness or focused interest as
obsession.

This reframing has substantial implications for software design. If miscommunication is mutual,
then empathy-building tools must address both directions. They must do more than describe
neurodivergent experiences to neurotypical audiences; they must also offer neurodivergent users
explicit models of how their words may be received by neurotypical readers, and they must teach
actionable bridging strategies that neither party is forced to abandon their natural style to adopt.
SpecBridge embodies this bidirectional ambition through its three-mode structure, where each
mode addresses a different aspect of the empathy gap.

In parallel with this conceptual foundation, recent technological advances have made it possible
to implement such an application without compromising user privacy. Apple’s Foundation
Models framework, introduced with iOS 26, exposes a fully on-device large language model that
can perform structured generation against a developer-defined schema. This means that sensitive
personal text — the kind of awkward email or ambiguous social message a user might paste into
a tone decoder — never leaves the device. The combination of Metal-accelerated graphics,
CoreHaptics, AVAudioEngine, and Foundation Models on a single device makes the present
project both technically novel and ethically tenable.

1.3 Problem Statement
Existing empathy-building tools for neurodivergent understanding are predominantly passive,
unidirectional, and non-experiential. They suffer from four interrelated weaknesses:
   •​ Passivity: They are consumed in the way a documentary is consumed — by reading or
      watching — rather than experienced through embodied interaction.
   •​ Unidirectionality: They typically address the neurotypical audience’s need to understand
      neurodivergence, while offering very little to neurodivergent users seeking to understand
      neurotypical communication patterns and adapt their own language.
   •​ Sensory abstraction: They describe sensory processing differences in clinical or
      anecdotal language but do not allow the user to feel the difference. A paragraph about
      fluorescent flicker is not the same as a screen that actually flickers.
   •​ Privacy compromise: Any AI-powered language tool that requires a user to upload
      private text to a remote server creates a chilling effect that suppresses the very candour
      the tool is meant to support.
There exists no single integrated platform that allows a user to (a) experience sensory overload
through real-time multi-modal simulation, (b) explore dual-perspective communication
breakdowns from both neurotypical and neurodivergent points of view, and (c) receive private,
on-device, AI-generated bridge-language suggestions — all within one accessible,
offline-capable application.

1.4 Need for the Proposed System
The need for SpecBridge arises from a confluence of social, educational, and technological
factors. Socially, awareness of neurodivergence in the workplace, in education, and in healthcare
settings is rising, but the toolkit available to organisations seeking to build inclusive cultures has
remained limited to slide decks and brief workshops. Educationally, teachers and school
counsellors require resources that translate research findings about sensory processing and
communication into experiences that students and staff can engage with directly.
Technologically, the recent maturation of on-device generative AI removes the privacy barriers
that previously prevented sensitive communication-coaching tools from being deployed
responsibly.

There is, furthermore, a specific need within the iOS developer ecosystem for an application that
demonstrates how the new Foundation Models framework can be combined with traditional
multi-modal frameworks — Metal, CoreHaptics, AVAudioEngine — to produce experiences that
are simultaneously emotionally meaningful and technically deep. SpecBridge addresses both the
human and the technical need.

Chapter 2: System Study / Literature Review
2.1 Introduction
A serious software project that engages with the lived experience of a marginalised population
must be grounded in the relevant academic, clinical, and self-advocacy literature. To design
SpecBridge responsibly, this chapter surveys the existing body of knowledge on neurodiversity,
communication breakdowns, sensory simulation, and on-device artificial intelligence. The
review serves three purposes: it justifies the conceptual framing of the application, it documents
the limitations of prior work that SpecBridge attempts to overcome, and it identifies the research
gap that the present project addresses. The chapter proceeds from broad context — the
neurodiversity paradigm — to the specific framework that drives the application’s three-mode
structure (the Double Empathy Problem), and from there into the technical literature on sensory
simulation systems and on-device language models that informed the implementation choices.

2.2 Overview of Neurodiversity Research
The term neurodiversity was coined by Australian sociologist Judy Singer in the late 1990s. The
framing was an intentional rhetorical departure from the medical-deficit model that had
previously dominated discussions of autism, ADHD, dyslexia, and related cognitive profiles.
Within the neurodiversity paradigm, these profiles are understood not as illnesses to be cured but
as naturally occurring variations in human cognition, each carrying its own pattern of strengths,
sensitivities, and communicative styles. The paradigm does not deny that neurodivergent
individuals face real challenges in environments designed around neurotypical defaults — it
relocates the source of those challenges from the individual to the mismatch between the
individual and the environment.

Subsequent empirical research has substantiated the paradigm’s core claims. Studies of sensory
processing have established that many autistic individuals experience genuine, measurable
hyper-reactivity to ordinary stimuli: heart-rate variability changes, cortisol levels rise, and
self-reported aversion intensifies in response to lighting, sound, and crowd densities that
neurotypical participants find unremarkable. Communication studies have shown that
neurodivergent communicative styles — directness, literalness, deep topical focus — are
internally consistent and effective among neurodivergent peers, but are systematically misread as
rude, evasive, or socially inept by neurotypical listeners. These findings provide the empirical
justification for the SpecBridge design: sensory and communicative differences are real, they are
mutual, and they are amenable to translation through carefully designed experiential tools.

2.3 The Double Empathy Problem
The Double Empathy Problem is a theoretical framework introduced by Dr. Damian Milton in
his 2012 paper “On the Ontological Status of Autism: The Double Empathy Problem.” Milton
argues that the empathy difficulties commonly observed in interactions between autistic and
non-autistic people are not the result of a unilateral deficit located in the autistic party. They are


                                               Page 15
instead the predictable result of two people from different communicative cultures attempting to
read one another’s minds and consistently misjudging the signal. Subsequent empirical work —
most notably the 2020 Crompton et al. studies of information transfer in autistic–autistic,
autistic–non-autistic, and non-autistic–non-autistic dyads — has confirmed that information loss
is highest in mixed dyads and equally low in matched dyads regardless of neurotype. The
conclusion: the problem lies in the gap, not in one side of it.

2.4 Existing Empathy-Building Tools and Their Limitations
Several classes of empathy-building tools currently exist, each with characteristic limitations.

Web-based awareness articles, such as those published by autism charities and disability
advocacy organisations, are widely available but suffer from the descriptive-versus-experiential
gap discussed earlier. Their reach is high but their depth of impact is low: readers can complete
an article and report no change in behaviour.

Documentary videos, including the well-known autism awareness films circulated on streaming
platforms, achieve higher emotional engagement than text but remain passive media. The viewer
observes the experience of others rather than entering into it. Interactive elements are typically
absent.

Virtual-reality sensory simulators, such as those developed for educator training, come closest to
the experiential ideal and have produced encouraging preliminary results. However, they require
dedicated hardware (a VR headset, a controlled room), are expensive to deploy at scale, and tend
to focus exclusively on the sensory dimension while neglecting the communicative dimension.

Clinical questionnaires and self-assessment tools provide a different kind of value — helping
neurodivergent users articulate their own profiles — but they are not designed for
empathy-building between groups. They produce inward-facing data, not outward-facing
experience.

Workshop-based interventions, common in corporate diversity and inclusion contexts, can be
powerful when led by skilled facilitators but are constrained by scheduling, geography, and
facilitator availability. They cannot be revisited at will by an individual learner.

No prior tool, to the author’s knowledge, integrates immersive multi-sensory simulation,
dual-perspective communication analysis, and AI-driven bridge-language generation in a single
application that runs fully on consumer mobile hardware without privacy compromise.

2.5 Sensory Simulation in HCI Research
Within the human–computer interaction (HCI) literature, sensory simulation has been explored
as a tool for accessibility training. Studies of stroboscopic and flicker effects have established
that pulsing overlays at frequencies between 3 and 12 Hz reliably produce subjective discomfort
in neurotypical participants, providing a measurable analogue to fluorescent-flicker sensitivity


                                               Page 16
reported by many autistic individuals. Studies of moiré and chromatic-aberration effects have
demonstrated that subtle red-blue offset, when combined with patterned backgrounds, produces a
measurable reduction in reading speed in neurotypical participants — a useful approximation of
the visual stress described by individuals with Irlen Syndrome and certain visual processing
differences. Studies of layered ambient noise have shown that the addition of low-frequency hum
and broadband white noise to a primary speech signal reduces speech intelligibility for all
listeners, providing a justification for the auditory-processing filter implemented in SpecBridge.

2.6 On-Device Language Models and Privacy
The introduction of Apple Foundation Models in iOS 26 changed this picture. The framework
exposes a compact, on-device language model with sub-second inference latency on modern
Apple Silicon and supports structured output generation through the @Generable schema
annotation. With Foundation Models, an application can perform sophisticated language analysis
without any network traffic, satisfying both privacy and offline-operability constraints.
SpecBridge’s Tone Decoder module is, to the author’s knowledge, one of the first educational
applications to demonstrate this pattern in the neurodiversity domain.

2.7 Research Gap
The research gap addressed by this project may be stated concisely. Despite a rich body of
literature establishing the Double Empathy Problem as the appropriate framework for
understanding neurotypical–neurodivergent communication, and despite the existence of mature
technologies for multi-sensory simulation and on-device AI, no integrated, accessible,
offline-capable mobile application currently combines all three capabilities. SpecBridge fills this
gap.

2.8 Summary
This chapter has surveyed the neurodiversity paradigm, the Double Empathy Problem, existing
empathy-building tools and their limitations, the HCI literature on sensory simulation, and the
recent emergence of on-device language models. The literature jointly supports the design
choices made in SpecBridge: a Double-Empathy-aware three-mode architecture, multi-modal
sensory simulation with safety bounds, on-device AI for privacy, and pre-authored fallback
content for accessibility on devices without Foundation Models support. The next chapter
formalises these findings into a system analysis and a requirements specification.

Chapter 3: System Analysis / Problem Description
3.1 Introduction
System analysis is the bridge between a research question and a buildable specification. Having
established in Chapters 1 and 2 that an integrated, on-device, multi-modal empathy tool is both
needed and feasible, this chapter formalises the requirements that the SpecBridge application
must satisfy. It begins with a survey of the systems already available to users seeking to
understand or to support neurodivergent communication, identifies the specific limitations of
each, articulates the proposed system in functional terms, and concludes with explicit functional
and non-functional requirements together with a feasibility study. The objective of the chapter is
to leave the reader in a position to evaluate the design and implementation chapters that follow
against a clearly stated specification.

3.2 Existing System Analysis
Five categories of existing system warrant examination, each representing a distinct point in the
design space of empathy-related software for neurodiversity.

Awareness websites — such as those operated by national autism societies and disability
advocacy non-profits — host text articles, blog posts, and downloadable PDFs explaining
neurodivergent experience. Their content is typically authored by clinicians, researchers, or
self-advocates and is updated on monthly to quarterly cycles. Their reach is broad but their
interactivity is essentially zero.

Video documentaries — distributed via mainstream streaming services and educational platforms
— provide higher emotional bandwidth than text but remain a one-way medium. A viewer can
pause, rewind, and re-watch, but cannot influence the trajectory of the content.

Virtual-reality sensory simulators — research prototypes developed in HCI laboratories and a
small number of commercial offerings aimed at educator training — place the user inside a 3D
environment whose sensory parameters can be modulated. They achieve high immersion but
require dedicated hardware, controlled physical space, and significant setup time. They are
typically licensed at institutional rather than individual price points.

Communication-coaching chatbots — built on top of cloud-hosted language models — analyse
user-supplied text and suggest alternative phrasings. They are functionally useful but their
reliance on remote inference creates a privacy barrier that is particularly problematic for the
kinds of messages users most need help with: messages from family, employers, and intimate
partners.

Workshop-based diversity-and-inclusion programmes — delivered in person or via webinar by
professional facilitators — combine narrative and small-group exercises. They are powerful




                                             Page 18
when well-led but expensive, geographically constrained, and impossible for an individual
learner to revisit at will.

3.3 Limitations of Existing Systems
Each of the five categories above exhibits one or more limitations that the proposed system
attempts to address.
   •​ Limitation L1 — Descriptive rather than experiential learning: Text and video media
      tell the user about neurodivergent experience but do not let them feel it.
   •​ Limitation L2 — Unidirectional empathy framing: Most tools train neurotypical users
      to understand neurodivergent users but offer no comparable resource in the opposite
      direction.
   •​ Limitation L3 — Hardware and venue barriers: VR-based simulators and
      workshop-based programmes both impose substantial logistical costs that exclude
      individual learners and informal contexts.
   •​ Limitation L4 — Privacy compromise: Cloud-based communication coaches require
      sensitive personal text to leave the user’s device.
   •​ Limitation L5 — Discontinuity: Existing tools are siloed — a sensory simulator does
      not connect to a communication coach, and a communication coach does not
      contextualise its advice within the user’s sensory experience.
   •​ Limitation L6 — Accessibility neglect: Many simulators ignore the Reduce Motion,
      Dynamic Type, and VoiceOver settings that some of their own intended users depend
      upon.

3.4 Proposed System
The proposed system, SpecBridge, is a single-binary iOS application that integrates three
interconnected modules to address the limitations identified above.

Module 1 — Sensory Simulator — addresses limitations L1 and L3. The user enters one of three
environment scenes (Classroom, Grocery Store, or Café) and proceeds through a five-step
interactive narrative during which the visual field is progressively distorted by combinations of
three sensory filters: Sensory Overload (flicker and noise), Visual Stress (chromatic aberration
and moiré patterns), and Auditory Processing (radial vignette and audio reverb). Each filter is
accompanied by a synchronised haptic pattern and an algorithmically synthesised audio bed. The
simulation runs entirely on the user’s phone and requires no additional hardware.

Module 2 — Perspective Bridge — addresses limitations L2 and L5. The user reads a short
surface conversation, then with a single tap reveals two side-by-side panels showing how a
neurotypical reader and a neurodivergent reader might interpret the same words. A third reveal
surfaces the specific communication pattern at the root of the divergence (for example, indirect


                                             Page 19
request, hedging, or social pleasantry), and a fourth presents a rewritten ‘bridge language’
alternative that works for both reading styles.

Module 3 — Tone Decoder — addresses limitation L4. The user pastes a message they have
received or are about to send, and the application uses Apple Foundation Models to analyse the
message on-device, producing the same dual-perspective breakdown that Module 2 presents on
pre-authored scenarios, this time generated dynamically for the user’s own content. Three
bridge-language rewrites are offered in three styles: Direct, Warm, and Detailed.

All three modules share a common navigation router, a common theme system with twelve
emotion-keyed colour palettes, and a common accessibility-respecting animation layer. Together
they constitute a coherent platform rather than a collection of disconnected utilities.

4.2 Overall System Architecture (MVVM)
SpecBridge adopts the Model–View–ViewModel (MVVM) architectural pattern, which is the de
facto standard for modern SwiftUI applications. The pattern was selected because it cleanly
separates concerns, makes view models unit-testable in isolation from UI, and integrates
naturally with SwiftUI’s @Observable property wrapper and reactive view system.

The Model layer encapsulates the application’s domain data: SensoryFilter and
EnvironmentScene enums, Scenario / Perspective / BridgeLanguage structs, BridgeAnalysis
result types, and the @Generable schema BridgeAnalysisResult used by Foundation Models. The
Model layer is pure Swift with no UI or framework dependencies beyond Foundation, allowing it
to be reasoned about independently of the rest of the application.

The ViewModel layer hosts the business logic and orchestrates the asynchronous flows between
user actions, audio engine state, haptic engine state, AI service responses, and UI presentation.
Notable view models include Mode1ViewModel (sensory scene state machine),
Mode2ViewModel (perspective reveal flow), and Mode3ViewModel (tone decoder request
lifecycle). All view models are marked @Observable and @MainActor, ensuring thread safety
and SwiftUI integration.

The View layer is built exclusively with SwiftUI: NavigationStack, TabView, custom
ViewModifiers, Canvas, TimelineView, KeyframeAnimator, PhaseAnimator, and MeshGradient.
There are no UIViewControllerRepresentable bridges in the production code path, ensuring
forward compatibility with future SwiftUI releases.

Cross-cutting concerns — theming, navigation routing, audio engine ownership, haptic engine
ownership, and AI availability — are encapsulated in a small number of singletons exposed via
the SwiftUI Environment: AppTheme, AppRouter, SensoryAudioEngine, HapticEngine, and
AIAvailabilityService.

4.4 Data Flow Design
Two principal data flows characterise the application’s                 runtime    behaviour:   the
sensory-simulation flow and the language-analysis flow.

The sensory-simulation flow proceeds as follows. The user selects a scene through
ScenePickerView, which invokes AppRouter to push the chosen SceneView. The SceneView
instantiates a Mode1ViewModel that holds the current step index and the corresponding (filter,
intensity) tuple. Each step’s onAppear triggers four parallel asynchronous tasks:
NarrationOverlay begins a StreamingTextView typewriter reveal; SensoryAudioEngine receives
a configure(filter:intensity:) call that updates the reverb / distortion wet/dry mix and the ambient
layer stack; HapticEngine receives a playPattern(for:intensity:) call that loads the matching
CHHapticPattern; and the SceneView’s sensoryEffect view modifier activates the visual filter at
the requested intensity. When the user taps the highlighted scene element, the view model
advances the step index and the cycle repeats.

The language-analysis flow proceeds as follows. The user enters text in ToneInputView and taps
‘Bridge This Message’. The Mode3ViewModel calls ToneAnalysisService.analyse(text:), which
checks AIAvailabilityService for the current AITier; on .foundationModels the service initialises
a    LanguageModelSession       with     the   bridge-analyst     instructions   and    invokes
session.respond(to:generating:BridgeAnalysisResult.self); the returned struct is mapped to the
BridgeAnalysis domain type and emitted to the view via the view model’s analysis
@Published-style property. On .fallbackOnly the service short-circuits to a pre-authored or

NLTagger-derived BridgeAnalysis and surfaces an unavailability banner. ToneResultView
observes the analysis and renders the three-page carousel.

4.5 UI/UX Design Principles
Five design principles guide the user interface throughout the application.
   •​ Progressive disclosure: complex content (filter intensities, perspective interpretations,
      AI rewrites) is revealed step-by-step rather than dumped onto the user.
   •​ Multi-sensory coherence: visual, auditory, and haptic channels always reinforce one
      another; the user is never asked to interpret a discordant signal.
   •​ Accessibility-first: every animation has a Reduce Motion fallback; every text element
      scales with Dynamic Type; every interactive element exceeds the 44 pt minimum touch
      target.
   •​ Emotional honesty: the application does not soften the discomfort of the sensory
      simulation, because the discomfort is the point — but it bounds the discomfort within
      safety limits and always provides an exit.
   •​ Privacy transparency: the application states plainly that no user content leaves the
      device, and visibly indicates the on-device AI availability tier.

4.6 Shader and Effects Pipeline Design
The visual effects are implemented as a stack of three SwiftUI ViewModifier types:
SensoryOverloadEffect, VisualStressEffect, and AuditoryProcessingEffect. Each modifier is
parameterised by an enabled flag, an intensity value between zero and one, and a reduceMotion
flag drawn from the SwiftUI Environment.

SensoryOverloadEffect composes a TimelineView-driven pulsing orange overlay using
blendMode(.plusLighter), a procedural noise field rendered through a Canvas (40–120 particles
depending on intensity), a contrast boost that scales with intensity, a saturation boost, and a
subtle scale effect (1.0 to 1.007) that contributes a slight disorientation cue.

VisualStressEffect composes a chromatic-aberration treatment in which red and blue
channel-tinted Color overlays are offset in opposite horizontal directions at a sinusoidal 3 Hz
frequency, a Canvas-rendered moiré pattern whose line count and phase scale with intensity, and
a soft blur and contrast lift.

AuditoryProcessingEffect composes a TimelineView-driven radial vignette pulsing at 4 Hz,
narrowing the visible region toward a centred focus point, and a moderate blur radius that scales
with intensity. The visual metaphor of the filter is the cognitive narrowing that accompanies the
effort of disentangling overlapping audio streams.

All    three modifiers are applied to the scene root through a single
sensoryEffect(filter:intensity:reduceMotion:) extension, ensuring that filter changes propagate
cleanly through SwiftUI’s diffing engine.

4.7 Audio Graph Design
The audio engine is built around AVAudioEngine and contains two parallel signal paths: a speech
path and an ambient path. The speech path runs AVSpeechSynthesizer.write(utterance:) to render
PCM buffers asynchronously, normalises those buffers to 44.1 kHz mono via AVAudioConverter,
schedules them on an AVAudioPlayerNode, then routes them through an AVAudioUnitReverb
(mediumRoom preset) and an AVAudioUnitDistortion (speechWaves preset) before feeding a
dedicated speechMixer node. The ambient path runs an AVAudioSourceNode whose render
callback synthesises layered sine, white-noise, and pulse-square waves at frequencies dictated by
the active filter; this node feeds a dedicated ambientMixer. The two mixer outputs are combined
in the engine’s mainMixer and routed to the device output. The AVAudioSession is configured
with the .playback category and the .mixWithOthers option, so that background music and
podcasts are never interrupted.

4.8 AI Integration Design
The Foundation Models integration is anchored by the AIAvailabilityService class, which
exposes a currentTier published property of type AITier. AITier has two cases:

from builds targeting older SDKs.

Mode 3’s ToneAnalysisService is the most fully developed AI integration in the application. It
initialises a LanguageModelSession with a 25-line system instruction that defines the
bridge-analyst role, lists the twelve valid emotion strings, describes the three bridging styles, and

5.6 Audio Engine Implementation
The SensoryAudioEngine file totals approximately 440 lines and is the most algorithmically
dense component of the application. It owns an AVAudioEngine instance and three persistent
processing nodes: an AVAudioUnitReverb, an AVAudioUnitDistortion, and a dedicated
AVAudioPlayerNode for speech playback. It additionally owns an AVAudioSourceNode whose
render callback performs lock-free, allocation-free ambient noise synthesis on the real-time audio
thread.

The ambient render state is held in a struct AmbientRenderState marked @unchecked Sendable,
containing a copy-on-write array of AmbientLayer instances and a continuous phase tracker. The
main thread snapshots the layer array on every filter change; the audio thread reads the most
recent snapshot without locks. The continuous phase tracker prevents click and pop artefacts at
filter transitions.

5.7 Haptic Engine Implementation

6.1 Introduction
Testing is the discipline of asking, methodically and with intent to fail, whether a system actually
meets its specification. For SpecBridge, the question is sharpened by the application’s
multi-modal, real-time, and accessibility-critical character: it is not enough that the code
compiles and the views render — the visual filters must hit their target frame rate, the haptic
patterns must synchronise within human-perceptible thresholds, the AI service must degrade
gracefully, and every interaction must remain operable for a user relying on VoiceOver and
Reduce Motion. This chapter describes the testing strategy that was adopted, the categories of
tests that were executed, the tooling used, and the results obtained.

6.2 Testing Strategy
Five categories of testing were planned at the outset of the project and carried out in stages
during the implementation period: unit testing of model and view-model code, integration testing
of the inter-module flows, performance testing of the visual and audio subsystems, accessibility
testing under each of the major iOS accessibility settings, and informal user acceptance testing
with a small cohort of volunteer participants.

Two principles guided the strategy. First, the simplest possible test that could falsify a claim was
preferred over a more elaborate test that could only confirm it. Second, tests were authored as the
corresponding production code was written, rather than retrofitted at the end, to ensure that test
failures reflected real regressions rather than test rot.

6.3 Unit Testing
Unit tests cover three principal areas: the model layer’s value-type behaviour, the audio engine’s

Integration tests exercise the inter-module flows that unit tests cannot reach. Four scenarios were

Foundation Models unavailable degradation: launching the application on an iPad without

Audio interruption handling: starting playback of background music, launching SpecBridge,

such as an incoming call.

6.5 Performance and Frame-Rate Testing

Performance testing was conducted using Instruments (Time Profiler, Core Animation, Metal
System Trace) on three target devices: iPhone 15 Pro (A17 Pro), iPhone 16 (A18), and iPad Air
M2. Frame-rate measurements were taken under five filter combinations: no filter, single filter at

advance and the corresponding CHHapticEvent dispatch. The mean delta was 2.8 milliseconds,
the 95th percentile was 4.1 milliseconds, and the maximum observed was 4.6 milliseconds — all
within the 5-millisecond NFR3 target.

6.6 Accessibility Testing

6.7 User Acceptance Testing
Informal user acceptance testing was conducted with a small cohort of six volunteer participants
spanning three demographic groups: two self-identified autistic adults, two neurotypical adults
with close family members on the autism spectrum, and two neurotypical adults with no prior
exposure to neurodiversity content. Each participant completed a 25-minute session in which
they navigated through each of the three modules and answered a brief structured questionnaire
afterward.

Across all six participants, the Perspective Bridge module was identified as the most
immediately actionable. Participants reported intending to apply the bridge-language rewrites in
their own writing within the following week. The Tone Decoder module was praised for its
on-device privacy guarantee; one participant explicitly mentioned that they would not have used
a cloud-based equivalent.

This chapter has documented the five-category testing strategy adopted for SpecBridge and the
results obtained. Unit tests passed across the model layer, the audio engine helpers, and the AI

sub-5-millisecond haptic synchronisation. Accessibility tests confirmed compliance with the six
major iOS accessibility settings. Informal user acceptance testing produced encouraging
qualitative feedback. The next chapter discusses these outcomes in greater depth and compares
the application’s performance to relevant existing systems.

7.2 Functional Outcomes

the originally specified items.

7.4 Qualitative User Feedback
The user acceptance testing reported in Section 6.7 yielded a set of qualitative observations that
bear directly on the project’s success criterion: did the application actually move users toward
empathic understanding? Three threads stood out in the post-session interviews.

First, participants consistently reported a ‘before-and-after’ shift in their conception of
neurodivergent experience. Even the autistic participants — for whom the simulator could not
deliver novel information — reported that the application gave them a vocabulary and a
shareable artefact for explaining their experience to neurotypical family members.

Second, participants praised the Tone Decoder’s privacy guarantee specifically. The on-device
inference label was noticed and valued; one participant explicitly stated that they would not have
used a cloud-based version of the same feature with personal email content.

Third, several participants requested an extended scene library — kitchen, public transit, doctor’s
waiting room — and suggested that the Tone Decoder be invokable from outside the app via a
Share Sheet extension. These suggestions are captured in the Future Scope section of Chapter 8.

7.6 Limitations
Several limitations of the project deserve explicit acknowledgement.

The simulation, by design, is bounded in intensity. It cannot replicate the full magnitude of
sensory overwhelm reported by some autistic individuals, nor can it reproduce the cumulative
fatigue of an entire day under a hostile sensory load. Users should leave the simulation
understanding that the experience they had was an approximation tuned to the safe operating
limits of the device and the average user’s tolerance.

The application has been tested on a small participant cohort (six volunteers). A larger and more
demographically diverse evaluation would substantially strengthen the qualitative claims made in
this chapter.

Chapter 8: Conclusion and Future Scope
8.1 Conclusion
The SpecBridge project set out to address a clearly identified gap in the landscape of
empathy-building software for neurotypical–neurodivergent communication: the absence of an
integrated, on-device, multi-modal tool that combines immersive sensory simulation with
dual-perspective communication analysis and privacy-preserving AI-driven language coaching.
The project has demonstrated, through the design, implementation, testing, and evaluation phases
documented in this report, that such a tool is technically feasible on contemporary consumer
mobile hardware and that it produces empathic outcomes that text-based and video-based
predecessors cannot match.

Three architectural choices proved central to this outcome. First, the decision to build directly on
top of Apple’s native multi-modal frameworks — Metal, SwiftUI, CoreHaptics, AVAudioEngine,
Foundation Models — rather than on a cross-platform abstraction layer, gave the project access
to the synchronisation guarantees, the on-device inference, and the accessibility integrations on
which its differentiation depends. Second, the decision to synthesise all audio at runtime rather
than ship a sample library kept the binary small, the experience procedurally responsive, and the
privacy story complete. Third, the decision to author three carefully designed pre-authored
Perspective Bridge scenarios and treat the Foundation Models dynamic-generation pathway as
augmentation rather than replacement ensured that the application remains pedagogically
valuable on every supported device.

The project has also confirmed, through user acceptance testing, that the Double Empathy
framing yields a more effective design than the conventional unilateral-deficit framing. Both
neurotypical and neurodivergent participants reported value from the application: the former
gained vocabulary for experiences they had not previously imagined, and the latter gained a
shareable artefact for explaining their experience to others. This bidirectional benefit is the
central practical achievement of the project.

8.2 Key Achievements
The principal achievements of the project may be enumerated as follows:
   •​ Delivery of a fully functional, single-binary iOS application that runs on any iPhone or
      iPad with iOS 18 or later, with no external dependencies.
   •​ Implementation of three sensory simulation filters as composable SwiftUI ViewModifier
      effects, each respecting the Reduce Motion accessibility setting and bounded within
      safe-flicker guidelines.
   •​ Implementation of an AVAudioEngine audio graph that synthesises speech distortion and
      ambient noise entirely at runtime, with zero external audio files and lock-free real-time
      render callbacks.


                                              Page 39
   •​ Implementation of three CoreHaptics patterns synchronised with the active sensory filter
      at sub-5-millisecond latency.
   •​ Authoring of three production-grade Perspective Bridge scenarios covering workplace,
      social, and educational contexts, each with full dual-perspective interpretation,
      miscommunication-point analysis, and bridge-language rewrite.
   •​ Implementation of a Tone Decoder module that uses Apple Foundation Models with a
      12-field @Generable schema to deliver dual-perspective analysis and three
      bridge-language rewrites on-device, with a fully working NLTagger-based and
      pre-authored fallback path.
   •​ Full compliance with the six major iOS accessibility settings (VoiceOver, Dynamic Type,
      Reduce Motion, Reduce Transparency, Increase Contrast, Bold Text).
   •​ Documentation of the project from problem framing through testing in approximately 40
      pages of academic-format prose, supporting future research and reproduction.

8.3 Future Scope
Future development of SpecBridge can be organised along three axes: breadth (more scenes,
more scenarios, more languages), depth (richer simulation, more sophisticated AI integration),
and reach (new platform surfaces and integration paths).

Along the breadth axis, the most immediate priority is the expansion of the scene library beyond
the present three environments. Public transit, doctor’s waiting rooms, restaurants, supermarkets
at peak hours, and open-plan offices were all requested by user acceptance test participants. Each
new scene requires composition of SwiftUI geometry and authoring of a step-by-step narrative;
the existing scene infrastructure is designed to absorb additions without architectural change.
Similarly, the Perspective Bridge scenario library should be expanded from three to a target of
fifteen scenarios, distributed across additional contexts including family relationships, healthcare
interactions, and dating. Localisation into Hindi, Spanish, Mandarin, and other major languages
is also a high-priority breadth item, requiring translation of all UI strings, narration content, and
scenario dialogue.

Along the depth axis, three directions warrant exploration. First, additional sensory profiles —
proprioceptive (body-awareness perturbation), vestibular (balance distortion), and interoceptive
(internal-sensation amplification) — would complement the present visual and auditory filters
and address sensory differences that are common in autistic and ADHD experience but presently
unsimulated. Second, the Mode 2 Foundation Models dynamic-scenario-generation pathway
should be activated, allowing the application to generate fresh scenarios on demand from short
user prompts. Third, integration with HealthKit could enable a personalised sensitivity
calibration step in which the application learns the user’s preferred intensity bounds and adjusts
the simulation accordingly.



                                               Page 40
Along the reach axis, three integrations would substantially extend the application’s impact. A
Share Sheet extension would allow users to invoke the Tone Decoder on any text they encounter
in Mail, Messages, or any other app, dramatically lowering the activation cost of using the
feature. A companion educator dashboard, delivered as a separate iPadOS app, would allow
teachers, trainers, and workshop facilitators to deploy scenarios and observe aggregate
(anonymous) engagement metrics from a class of users. A visionOS adaptation would explore
whether the higher immersion of spatial computing enhances the empathy outcome relative to
phone-based use.

Each of these expansion paths preserves the core architectural and ethical commitments of the
present project: on-device processing, accessibility-first design, free-to-the-user distribution, and
grounding in published neurodiversity research.

8.4 Final Remarks
Software, at its best, does more than implement features. It encodes a stance toward the world
and toward the people the software is meant to serve. The stance encoded in SpecBridge is that
miscommunication between neurotypical and neurodivergent people is a mutual problem, that it
is amenable to translation, that translation begins with felt experience rather than with
description, and that the privacy of the individual is a non-negotiable foundation on which
empathy-building tools must rest.

If this project has succeeded, it has done so not by adding another awareness article to an already
crowded literature, but by making available, to anyone with a supported phone, a small set of
carefully designed experiences and tools that did not previously exist as an integrated whole. The
author hopes that future research will extend, critique, and replace what is described here, and
that the framing of mutual bridging — rather than unilateral cure — will continue to shape the
design of the next generation of inclusion-oriented software.
